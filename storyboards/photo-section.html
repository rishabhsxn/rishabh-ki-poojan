Little touches that tie it to the site

  • The ring is marigolds. The cover's story ring is a string of marigolds, like the garland over the palace. It turns slowly, and once someone has watched to the end it fades to soft gold, the way a watched story's ring greys out.
  • Your names and the kalash. The round picture beside "Rishabh & Poojan" is the site's own kalash icon on maroon.
  • Gold bars and captions. The progress bars fill in gold, and captions sit low in the site's italic serif under a short gold rule.
  • A slow drift. Each photo zooms in very slightly while it shows, so nothing sits completely still.
  • Opens from the cover. The viewer grows out of the cover and shrinks back into it. With the arch cover, the arch flattens into the screen edge as it grows.
  • A closing card. After the last photo: your monogram, the date, "Thank you for being part of our story", Watch again and Close, with a few marigold petals drifting down.
  • The song. The speaker in the viewer is the site's own mute button, so the song can be silenced without closing.

On a computer: four ways

A phone is tall, so a stories card fills it naturally. A computer screen is wide, and your photos are a mix of portrait and landscape, so the same card would leave a lot of empty space. Each option below opens from the same cover and keeps the same gold bars, names, captions and closing card. Clicking the right or left side of the photo moves on or back, the arrow keys work, and Esc closes.

My pick on a computer: D3, the card that changes shape

It solves the real problem a wide screen has with your photos, the mix of portrait and landscape: each photo is as large as the screen allows, with nothing cropped and no empty bands. It keeps the feel of the phone version, with the same bars, header, taps and closing card. If you would rather the computer look exactly like the phone, D1 is the safe choice.

The rickshaw lane, sideways only

Swipe sideways to ride between the doorways. Swiping up, or scrolling down, goes straight on to the next section. The dots stay; the side arrows are gone.

  • The lane becomes one screen tall. Today it holds the page for about five screens of scrolling while it rides; now scrolling passes it like any other section.
  • On a phone, the direction of a swipe is decided in its first few millimetres, so a slightly diagonal swipe still does the right thing.
  • On a computer: a trackpad's sideways swipe, dragging with the mouse, the ← → keys, or clicking a dot. A plain mouse wheel only scrolls down, so on a computer I would have the dots show the function names when you point at them.
  • The risk: a guest who only scrolls down will see the blessings and pass all four functions. Three things guard against that, all in the preview: the next doorway peeks in at the right edge, the lane nudges sideways once when it arrives, and a hint stays up until the first swipe.
  • The rickshaw still pedals as the lane moves.
